# Fire Card

Fire gives warmth, safe water, cooked food, light, a signal and a lift in spirits. Make it small, keep it under control, and never inside a tent or closed room: carbon monoxide kills.

## Before you light it
- **Pick the spot**: sheltered from wind, away from overhanging branches and dry grass. Scrape a circle down to bare dirt, about 10 feet (3 m) across. On snow or wet ground, build on a platform of green sticks.
- Keep water or dirt within reach.
- **Gather everything first, twice as much as you think**:
  - **Tinder** catches a spark: dry grass, birch bark, cattail fluff, pine needles, dryer lint, cotton balls rubbed with petroleum jelly, char cloth, resin-soaked pine (fatwood).
  - **Kindling**: dry twigs from pencil-lead to thumb thickness. Dead branches still on the tree are drier than ones on the ground.
  - **Fuel**: wrist-thick wood and bigger. Split wet logs to reach the dry wood inside.

## Ways to light it, easiest first
- **Lighter or matches.** Keep them in a waterproof bag. Stormproof matches keep burning in wind.
- **Ferro rod**: scrape it hard with the back of a knife or a striker, right into the tinder.
- **Flint and steel**: hard stone (flint, chert or quartz) struck against high-carbon steel throws sparks; catch them on char cloth or tinder fungus, then fold it into a tinder bundle and blow gently. Two ordinary rocks won't make usable sparks.
- **Sunlight**: a magnifying glass, reading glasses for farsightedness, a clear bottle or plastic bag full of water, or the polished bottom of a can, focused to the smallest bright point on dark tinder.
- **Battery and steel wool**: touch both terminals of a 9-volt battery (or two AA batteries end to end) to fine steel wool. It glows instantly. Keep them apart until you need it.
- **Bow drill** (takes practice): a spindle and a flat board of the same dry softwood (cedar, willow, cottonwood, basswood). Cut a notch in the board's edge. Wrap the bow's cord once around the spindle, press down on its top with a block of wood or a stone with a dimple, and saw back and forth steadily. When the notch fills with dark dust and keeps smoking, you have an ember: tip it into the tinder bundle and blow gently until it flames.

## Building it
- **Teepee**: tinder in the middle, kindling leaned around it. Lights fast, burns hot.
- **Log cabin**: kindling stacked in squares around a small teepee. Lasts longer and cooks well.
- Add wood gradually, small to big; fire needs air as much as fuel.
- A wall of logs or rocks behind the fire throws heat toward your shelter. Don't use rocks from rivers or wet ground: trapped water can make them crack or burst.
- Cook over glowing coals, not tall flames.

## Keeping it
- At night, bank the coals with ashes; they'll be alive in the morning.
- Carry embers in a tin with dry, crumbly rotten wood or tinder fungus to start the next fire.

## Signal fires
Three fires in a triangle mean distress. Green leaves make white smoke that shows against forest; rubber or oil makes black smoke that shows against snow and sand. Keep signal fires built and ready, and light them when you hear an aircraft.

## Putting it out
Drown it with water, stir the ashes, drown it again, and feel for heat with the back of your hand. If it's too hot to touch, it's too hot to leave.

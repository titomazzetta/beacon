# Sea, Island and Water Crossing Card

## In the water
- **Keep your life jacket on.** Float on your back to rest; anything that floats helps.
- **Cold water**: the first minute of gasping passes, so keep your head up and get your breathing under control. You have roughly 10 minutes of useful movement before your arms and legs stop working, so use it to get out or onto something that floats. Then pull your knees to your chest (or huddle with others) to hold heat.
- Caught in a current pulling you from shore (a rip current): swim **parallel to the beach** until you're out of it, then swim in. Don't fight it head-on.
- Don't swim for a distant shore unless you're sure you can make it. Stay with the boat or anything floating; it's far easier to spot than a swimmer.

## Adrift on a raft or boat
- **Stay with the craft** and stay together. Rig shade, keep clothes on, rest during the heat of the day, and move as little as possible to save water.
- **Never drink seawater or urine.** Catch every drop of rain with tarps, sails and containers. Ration the fresh water you have.
- Eat only if you have water: digesting protein (fish, birds) uses water.
- **Signal** when a ship or aircraft is actually in sight: mirror, flares, a light at night, bright cloth, a whistle. A PLB or EPIRB alerts rescuers right away (see the Satellite and car card).

## Stranded on an island or remote coast
1. **Safety and first aid** first: get everyone out of the water and the sun.
2. **Shelter** above the high-tide line (the line of dry seaweed and debris shows it). Stay clear of coconut palms; falling nuts injure people.
3. **Water**: catch rain with leaves, tarps and anything that holds water. Green coconuts hold safe water; the milk of ripe brown coconuts acts as a laxative in large amounts. Behind the first sand dune you can dig down to damp sand and fresh water may seep in. Taste it: if it's salty, don't drink it.
4. **Fire** for warmth, cooking and signaling (see the Fire card).
5. **Signals**: SOS or HELP in big letters of rocks, logs or seaweed on open sand, a signal fire ready to light, a mirror.
6. **Food**: fish, crabs, clams and mussels from clean water (never during a red tide, and never shellfish that smell bad or were already dead), coconuts, sea birds and their eggs. Avoid pufferfish, and large reef predators like barracuda, which can carry ciguatera poison.
- **Stay or go?** If anyone knows roughly where you are, stay put and signal. People are usually found, not self-rescued.

## Rafts and crossings
- **River or lagoon**: cross at the widest, shallowest, slowest stretch. Unbuckle your pack's hip belt, face upstream at an angle, and use a pole for a third leg. Never cross floodwater.
- **Floats**: empty bottles or jugs tied together, dry logs, or your pack wrapped tightly in a tarp or trash bags.
- **Log raft**: dry, light logs lashed together, with cross-poles on top and bottom. Test it in shallow water first.
- **Open sea**: an improvised raft or "do-it-yourself sailboat" can't sail upwind; a tarp on a mast only pushes you downwind. Leaving by sea in something improvised is a last resort for when staying means certain death. If you must, go only toward land you can see, in calm weather and daylight, with flotation for everyone, all the water you can carry, and a way to signal.

## Fishing with what you have
- **Hooks** from wire, safety pins, a soda-can tab, thorns or bone. A **gorge** works too: a sliver of bone or hard wood sharpened at both ends, tied at the middle and hidden in bait.
- **Line** from the inner strands of paracord, thread or plant fiber. **Bait**: worms, insects, shellfish, and the guts of the first fish you catch.
- Fish at dawn and dusk, in shade, near rocks and fallen trees. In tidal pools, a low wall of stacked rocks traps fish as the tide goes out.
- Spearing in shallow water: aim below where the fish appears; water bends the light.
- Check local rules where they still apply. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C).

## Finding land at sea
Clouds that sit still over one spot, and a greenish tint under clouds, often mean land or a lagoon. Seabirds fly out to sea in the morning and back toward land at dusk. Floating plants and debris increase near land.

Sources: US Army ATP 3-50.21 Survival and FM 3-05.70 (sea survival, water crossings); US Coast Guard; National Center for Cold Water Safety; NOAA; CDC (ciguatera and shellfish poisoning).

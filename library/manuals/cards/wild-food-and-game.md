# Wild Food, Hunting and Fishing Card

## The one rule
**If you're not 100% sure what it is, don't eat it.** Learn the edible plants of your own region from a regional field guide *before* you need them, ideally with someone who knows them.

## Never eat
- **Wild mushrooms**, unless an expert identified them in person. The deadliest (death cap, destroying angel) look ordinary, taste fine, and symptoms show up 6–24 hours later, after the liver damage is done.
- Plants in the **carrot / parsley family with umbrella-shaped white flower clusters**: poison hemlock and water hemlock are among the most toxic plants in North America and look like wild carrot.
- Plants with **milky sap** (except ones you know, like dandelion), a **bitter or soapy taste**, or an **almond smell**.
- **Shiny three-leaved plants** (poison ivy, poison oak).
- **Yew** (needles and seeds are deadly). **Death camas** looks like wild onion but has **no onion smell**: if it doesn't smell like onion, it isn't one.

## Common, widespread, easy to recognize (still confirm with a guide)
- **Dandelion**: leaves, flowers, roots; all edible.
- **Cattail**: young shoots and rhizomes (starchy roots). Grows in wet ground.
- **Blackberries and raspberries** (berries made of many little bubbles).
- **Clover** and **plantain** (*Plantago*, the lawn weed) leaves.
- **Acorns**: crack, then soak or boil in changes of water until no longer bitter, to leach out tannins.
- **Wild onion and garlic**: only if it smells strongly of onion.
- **Pine needle tea** (vitamin C). Avoid if pregnant, and be sure it's not yew.

## The "universal edibility test"
The Army survival manual describes a step-by-step test that takes more than 24 hours. It **doesn't work for mushrooms** and won't protect you from the most poisonous plants. Treat it as an absolute last resort, never a shortcut.

## Hunting and trapping
- Follow your state's seasons, licenses and rules; in a true emergency, the law still applies until it doesn't.
- **Don't eat animals that act sick**, are found dead, or look abnormal. Deer and elk can carry chronic wasting disease (CWD): don't eat meat from an animal that looked sick, and test where testing is available.
- Wear gloves to clean **rabbits and squirrels** (tularemia risk) and cook them thoroughly.
- Field-dress quickly and **cool the meat fast**: get it below 40°F (4°C) within a few hours.
- **Bear and wild pig** can carry trichinella: cook to at least **160°F (71°C)** with no pink.
- Snares and deadfalls are illegal in many places outside a genuine survival situation. They're also dangerous to pets and people.

## Fishing
- Cook fish to **145°F (63°C)**, until it flakes and is no longer translucent.
- Don't harvest **clams, mussels or oysters** where there are shellfish warnings or red tide. Their toxins aren't destroyed by cooking and can paralyze.
- Simple gear goes a long way: hooks, line, sinkers, and worms or insects for bait. Fish early morning and evening, near structure, in shade and where currents meet.

## Insects
Grasshoppers, crickets and grubs are high in protein. **Cook them** to kill parasites. Avoid brightly colored, hairy, or bad-smelling insects.

## Food handling without a fridge
Keep raw meat away from everything else. Wash hands with soap. Cook thoroughly; eat or preserve leftovers quickly. Drying, smoking, salting, and canning (follow USDA methods exactly) preserve food.

Sources: US Army ATP 3-50.21 Survival; USDA FSIS safe minimum cooking temperatures; CDC (CWD, tularemia, trichinellosis, shellfish poisoning); state wildlife agencies.

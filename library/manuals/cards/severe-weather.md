# Severe Weather Card

**Watch** = conditions are right for it; get ready. **Warning** = it's happening or about to; act now.

## Tornado
- **Shelter**: lowest floor, small interior room or hallway with no windows (closet, bathroom, under stairs). Get under something sturdy. Cover your head and neck with arms, a mattress, helmets or pillows.
- **Mobile homes are not safe.** Go to a sturdy building or storm shelter *before* the storm arrives.
- **In a car**: don't hide under an overpass. If you have time and traffic allows, **drive to the nearest sturdy building**. If debris starts flying, pull over and park: either stay buckled in with your head below the windows, covered with your hands or a blanket, or get out and lie in a spot noticeably lower than the road, covering your head.
- **Signs**: dark greenish sky, large hail, a loud roar like a freight train, a low rotating cloud, debris in the air.

## Lightning
- **When thunder roars, go indoors.** Stay inside until **30 minutes after the last thunder**.
- No place outside is safe. Get into a building or a hard-topped car. Avoid hilltops, open fields, lone trees, water and metal fences.
- **Distance**: count seconds between flash and thunder. ÷ 5 = miles, ÷ 3 = kilometers. Under 30 seconds = you're in range.

## Hurricane and flood
- Know if you're in an evacuation zone. **Evacuate when told.** Storm surge, the ocean pushed ashore, causes most hurricane deaths.
- Shelter in an interior room on the lowest floor that won't flood. The calm eye passes; the other side of the storm is coming.
- **Turn Around, Don't Drown.** 6 inches of moving water can knock you down. 12 inches can carry away most cars. 2 feet can carry away SUVs and trucks.
- After: assume flood water is contaminated (sewage, fuel, chemicals) and may hide downed power lines. Boil or treat drinking water until told it's safe.

## Heat wave and high humidity
- **Humidity is the hidden danger**: when sweat can't evaporate, your body can't cool itself. Watch the heat index, not just the temperature.
- Drink water regularly before you're thirsty. Add electrolytes if you're sweating a lot.
- Do hard work early morning or evening. Rest in shade often.
- Above the mid-90s °F (about 35°C), **fans alone won't prevent heat illness**. Cool showers, wet cloths and a cool public building help.
- **Never leave children, older adults or pets in a parked car**, even for a minute.
- Check on older neighbors twice a day. Heat stroke is an emergency (see the First aid card).

## Winter storm and extreme cold
- Stay off roads if you can. If you must drive, carry the winter car kit (see the Car card).
- Dress in layers: wicking base, insulating middle, wind and waterproof shell. Hat, gloves, dry socks.
- Never heat with a stove, oven, grill or generator indoors (carbon monoxide). See the Power outage card.
- **Frostbite** shows first on fingers, toes, ears, nose and cheeks: white or grayish, numb, waxy.

## Earthquake
- **Drop, Cover, Hold On.** Get under a sturdy table and hold on. Stay away from windows. Don't run outside during the shaking, and don't stand in a doorway.
- After: expect aftershocks. Check for gas leaks: smell or hiss means get out and leave the door open. Don't use matches or switches.

## Wildfire and smoke
- **Leave early** when told, or sooner if you feel unsafe. Roads jam and fires move faster than you think.
- Smoke: stay indoors with windows closed, run a HEPA filter if you have one, and wear an **N95** mask outside. Cloth masks don't block smoke.

## Reading the sky (no forecast available)
- **Falling air pressure** (a barometer, or ears popping in a still room) and thickening clouds from the west usually mean bad weather within 12–24 hours.
- A **halo** around the sun or moon (thin high clouds) often comes before rain or snow within a day.
- **Tall puffy clouds building fast** in the afternoon, especially with flat dark bases or an anvil top = thunderstorms soon.
- "Red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky in the morning, sailors take warning" is roughly right where weather moves west to east, as in the US and Europe.

Sources: National Weather Service (NOAA) tornado, lightning, flood, heat and winter safety; FEMA / Ready.gov; CDC heat guidance; Earthquake Country Alliance.

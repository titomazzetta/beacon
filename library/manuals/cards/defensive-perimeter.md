# Defensive Perimeter Card (Home or Camp)

The goal is that trouble **passes you by**, and if it doesn't, you see it early and have time to act. Think in four layers, from the outside in.

## 1. Deter: look occupied, not worth it
- Lights on timers, a vehicle in the drive, a dog that barks. Signs of alertness, not signs of supplies.
- **Don't advertise what you have.** In a long emergency, light, generator noise and cooking smells draw attention. Cover windows at night; run generators in short, predictable windows or not at all.
- Organize with neighbors. A street that watches together is a much harder target than one house alone.

## 2. Detect: see them before they reach you
- **Early warning**: motion lights, battery driveway alarms, doorbell cameras, gravel on paths, and a dog. Cans or bells on a fence line are a no-power alarm.
- **Observation post**: pick spots that see the approaches (driveway, back fence, road) without being obvious. Upstairs windows work at home; slightly higher ground with cover works at camp.
- **Sectors**: give each watcher a clear area (front and left, back and right) so nothing is missed.
- **Watch schedule**: 2-hour shifts work well. At night, keep two people awake if you have enough. Hand over in person: what you saw, what changed.
- **Night vision**: no bright light at the watch post. Use a dim red light, and let your eyes adjust for 20–30 minutes.

## 3. Delay: make it slow and noisy to get in
- Solid doors with deadbolts. Reinforce strike plates with **3-inch screws** into the frame. Bar sliding doors with a dowel in the track.
- Lock gates and windows; trim bushes that hide doors and windows; put thorny plants under ground-floor windows.
- Vehicles, fencing and furniture can block a driveway or a single entry point. Keep **two ways out** clear for yourselves at all times.

## 4. Respond: decided in advance, not in the moment
- **Everyone knows the plan**: who calls 911, who takes the children to the safe room, where you meet.
- **Safe room**: a room with a solid door, a lock, a phone or radio, a light, water and the first-aid kit.
- **Signals**: agree on simple words or whistle codes. One long blast = come to the safe room. Three = emergency, help.
- **Fallback and escape**: know where you go if the house or camp has to be given up, and the route there. Leaving is always an option. Property isn't worth a life.
- **Never chase** anyone off your property. Never leave the safe room to confront someone.

## Camp perimeter (in the field)
- Camp away from roads and trails, out of sight lines, on higher ground that won't flood, at least 200 feet (60 m) from water.
- Keep fires small and shielded, or skip them. Smoke, light and noise carry far, especially at night.
- Store food, trash and toiletries away from tents: hung, or in bear canisters, 200 feet downwind.
- Agree on a rally point if the group is split up, and post a watch if the situation calls for it.

## Hard limits
- **No booby traps or spring guns.** They're illegal almost everywhere, and they injure family members, neighbors, children and firefighters. Courts have held owners liable.
- Use of force is governed by law even in emergencies, and the law varies by state and country. De-escalate, retreat when you can, and only defend life when there's no other choice.
- Firearms: see the Firearms card. Locked storage matters even more when stress is high and children are home.

Sources: US Army FM 3-21.75 (security, observation and sectors), TC 3-21.76 Ranger Handbook (security priorities); US Department of Justice Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design; FEMA / Ready.gov; National Park Service food-storage guidance.

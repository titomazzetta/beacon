# Satellite, GPS and Car Card

When cell towers and the internet are down, three things often still work: the GPS in your phone, satellites overhead, and the car in your driveway.

## GPS works without cell service
- A phone's GPS listens to satellites directly. It needs no cell signal and no internet, just a view of the sky. Without data, the first fix can take several minutes.
- **Download offline maps now**, while you have internet (Apple Maps and Google Maps both offer offline areas). Beacon's Map works offline too.
- **Read your coordinates**: on iPhone, open the Compass app (Location Services on); they're at the bottom. In Google Maps, touch and hold the map where your blue dot is. Type them into Beacon's Map.
- GPS keeps working in airplane mode on most phones, which saves a lot of battery.
- On the Beacon computer, a Meshtastic radio with GPS (like a T-Echo or T1000-E) can give the Mac its location: **Map > Use this device's location**.

## Text for help by satellite
- Many recent phones can reach emergency services by satellite when there's no signal: iPhone 14 and later (Emergency SOS, Messages and Find My via satellite), most Pixel 9 and later models (Satellite SOS), some Galaxy phones on Verizon, and many phones through T-Mobile's satellite texting. Coverage is the US, Canada and parts of Europe, Australia, Japan and other regions, not the whole world.
- How: call 911 (or your local number). If there's no service, the phone offers satellite and shows you where to point it. Go outside, away from trees and buildings, and hold still. Messages can take a minute or more.
- **Try the demo now** in your phone's emergency settings, so you know the steps before you need them.

## Satellite devices
- **Personal locator beacon (PLB)**: sends your position to rescuers through the international Cospas-Sarsat system. No subscription, one-way, only for life-threatening emergencies. Register it (free in the US through NOAA).
- **Satellite messengers** (Garmin inReach, ZOLEO and similar): two-way texts, location sharing and SOS to a 24/7 rescue center. They need a subscription.
- **Satellite internet** (Starlink): full internet where service is offered, as long as the dish has power: roughly 25 to 40 W for the Mini, more for the standard dish. Check it in **Tools > Power budget**.

## If you're hiding
Any transmitter (phone, radio, satellite dish) can be detected and located. Keep transmissions short, turn devices fully off between uses, and move after transmitting. See the Phone safety card.

## Your car: radio, charger and shelter
- **Radio**: AM and FM stations carry emergency alerts. Some AM stations have backup power to stay on the air in a national emergency (FEMA's Primary Entry Point stations). Scan both bands. Car radios can't receive NOAA Weather Radio (162 MHz); you need a weather radio for that.
- **Charging**: phone chargers in the 12 V socket or USB ports. A small plug-in inverter (about 150 W) runs a laptop or small devices. Bigger loads need an inverter clamped to the battery, with the engine running.
- **Battery**: a car battery has only a few hundred watt-hours you can safely use, and you need some left to start the engine. Run the engine for a while to recharge.
- **Heat**: run the engine only in the open, never in a garage, and keep the tailpipe clear of snow. Stranded in winter: run the engine and heater about 10 minutes each hour with a window cracked.
- **Signals**: hazard lights, three blasts on the horn, the hood raised, a bright cloth tied to the antenna.

## Other radios
NOAA weather radio (seven channels, 162.400 to 162.550 MHz), walkie-talkies (FRS and GMRS), ham radio and mesh radios: see the Radio and Mesh radio cards.

Sources: Apple, Google and carrier documentation; NOAA SARSAT; FEMA (IPAWS and Primary Entry Point stations); National Weather Service; Maine Emergency Management Agency; Starlink and manufacturer specifications. Phone features and coverage change: check your own phone.

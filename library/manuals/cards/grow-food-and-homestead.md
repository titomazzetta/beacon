# Grow Food and Homestead Card

## Start with a reality check
Growing all of a family's food takes land, water, tools, skill and several seasons of practice, and crops fail. Start now, start small, and keep stored food while you learn. Your local Cooperative Extension office knows your soil, climate and frost dates; Beacon's **Food garden for where you are** and **Homestead** plans fill in the details.

## Where to grow
- Most vegetables need **6 to 8 hours of direct sun**. Start with one bed (10 by 10 feet is plenty the first year), or containers on a balcony.
- Learn your **last spring frost and first fall frost dates**. They set your planting calendar.

## Soil
- **Compost**: mix "greens" (kitchen scraps, grass clippings, manure from plant-eating animals) with "browns" (dry leaves, straw, cardboard). Keep it as damp as a wrung-out sponge and turn it now and then. It's ready in a few months when it's dark and crumbly.
- **Mulch** with straw or leaves to keep moisture in and weeds down.
- Poor or contaminated ground: build raised beds and fill them with soil and compost. A soil test through the extension office is cheap.

## Grow calories, not just salad
- **Calorie crops**: potatoes, sweet potatoes, winter squash, dry beans, corn, sunflowers.
- **Nutrition**: greens (kale, collards, chard), tomatoes, onions, carrots, peppers.
- **Perennials** take years to start producing, so plant them early: fruit and nut trees, berries, asparagus, rhubarb.

## Seeds
- Choose **open-pollinated** (heirloom) varieties if you want to save seed: their seeds grow true. Hybrid (F1) seeds don't.
- Easiest to save: beans, peas, tomatoes and lettuce.
- Dry seeds well and store them cool, dark and dry in sealed jars. Most keep for several years. Save extra of what grows well where you live.

## Water
- **Rain**: one inch of rain on 1,000 square feet of roof is about 600 gallons. Rain barrels need screened lids (mosquitoes) and ideally a first-flush diverter.
- Drip lines and thick mulch use far less water than sprinklers. Water deeply and less often, early in the day.
- Rainwater for drinking must be filtered and disinfected (see the Water card).

## Animals (where allowed)
- **Chickens**: eggs (about 3 to 6 a week per hen, depending on breed, age and daylight), pest control, manure for compost. They need a predator-proof coop.
- **Rabbits**: quiet, efficient meat; their manure goes straight on the garden.
- **Goats**: milk and brush clearing, but they need strong fencing and company.
- **Bees**: pollination and honey; learn from a local club first.
- Every animal needs daily water, feed, shelter and care, including during the emergency you're preparing for.

## Keep the harvest
- **Canning**: follow USDA-tested recipes exactly. Low-acid foods (vegetables, beans, meat) need a **pressure canner**; a water bath is only for high-acid foods (fruit, pickles, tomatoes with added acid). Botulism has no smell or taste.
- **Drying**: sun, oven or dehydrator, for fruit, vegetables, herbs and jerky.
- **Root cellar**: cool, dark and humid for potatoes, carrots, squash and cabbage. Keep apples apart: they make other produce spoil faster.
- **Fermenting**: sauerkraut is just cabbage and salt (about 3 tablespoons of salt per 5 pounds of cabbage).
- Dry grains and beans keep for years in sealed containers, cool and dry.

## Living off the grid
- **Water**: a well, a spring or rain catchment, with a way to filter and disinfect.
- **Power**: solar panels and batteries cover lights, phones, a fridge and tools (see the Off-grid power card).
- **Heat**: a wood stove. Cut and split firewood a year ahead so it's dry.
- **Sanitation**: an outhouse or composting toilet at least 200 feet (60 m) from any well or stream (see the Sanitation card).
- **Hand tools** and a way to sharpen them. Neighbors to trade with and share heavy work.

## The first year
Winter: plan, order seeds, start compost. Spring: plant after the last frost. Summer: water, weed, watch for pests. Fall: harvest, preserve, save seeds, plant garlic.

Sources: USDA and Cooperative Extension; National Center for Home Food Preservation (USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning); Texas Water Development Board, Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting; Peace Corps irrigation manual; FAO.
